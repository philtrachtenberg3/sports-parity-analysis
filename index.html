Money buys titles.
The question is how much parity you're allowed to keep.

American leagues cap spending and split national revenue evenly. Europe's top football leagues do neither. Sixteen seasons of champions, salary structures, and wage bills across nine leagues show exactly what that difference is worth.

01 — The Rules Each League Plays By

Same sport, entirely different economics

Every league in this analysis chases the same thing — a compelling season. But they're built on three different financial philosophies: hard caps with shared revenue, soft caps with tax penalties, and open markets with no cap at all.

04 — Title Concentration

The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, applied to trophies

Economists use HHI to measure market concentration — the same math that flags monopolies. Applied to championships (sum of each team's title-share, squared), a perfectly even 16-way split would score ~625. A single team winning every year scores 10,000.

Championship HHI by league, 2010–2025
Lower = titles spread across more teams. Higher = one or two teams dominate.
Bundesliga's HHI of 6,797 is almost entirely Bayern Munich: 13 titles in 16 years. Compare that to the NFL and NHL, both sitting at 1,172 — their most successful team over the same period won 3 titles, same as several others in the league.

06 — What Parity Looks Like Up Close

Small markets, big trophies — and the exceptions that prove the rule

Revenue sharing and hard caps don't just create more champions on paper — they let franchises in small media markets compete for sustained stretches without simply outspending the rest of the league.

NFL · Small Market
Kansas City & Buffalo
Two of the NFL's smallest media markets have combined for 4 Super Bowl titles and 8 conference championship appearances since 2020 — spending the exact same salary cap dollar-for-dollar as the Giants or Cowboys.
NBA · Small Market
OKC, Denver, Milwaukee
Three of the last five NBA champions (Thunder, Nuggets, Bucks) came from metro areas smaller than every top-5 European football club's home city. All built through the draft and cap-managed contracts, not outspending.
MLB · Small Market
Tampa Bay & Milwaukee
The Rays have made the playoffs 7 times since 2010 on bottom-5 payrolls almost every year. Milwaukee has been a perennial contender on a mid-size-market budget — for now. MLB's lack of a hard cap is this list's asterisk.
Premier League · The Anomaly
Leicester City, 2016
Priced at 5,000-to-1 by bookmakers, Leicester's title remains the single biggest upset in the dataset — and virtually the only one. It hasn't happened again since, and Leicester was relegated within 8 years.
Premier League · The Wealth Effect
Manchester City, 2012–2024
8 titles in 13 seasons after a 2008 takeover backed by Abu Dhabi's Sheikh Mansour turned City from mid-table into the league's benchmark spender — with no cap in place to slow it down.
Bundesliga · The Ceiling
Bayern Munich, 2013–2025
13 titles in 16 years, including a 10-year unbroken streak from 2013–2023. Bayern's revenue and wage bill run roughly 10x some of its rivals — there is no mechanism in German football to close that gap.
07 — Methodology & Caveats

How this was built

What's measured

Champion (league title/Finals winner) recorded for each of 16 seasons, 2010–2025, for the NFL, NBA, MLB, NHL, and Europe's top 5 football leagues (Premier League, La Liga, Bundesliga, Serie A, Ligue 1). Distinct-champion counts and HHI are computed directly from that list. Spend-spread figures use the most recently available full-season payroll or wage-bill data per league (2024–25 or 2025–26), not a 16-year average — payroll history at that granularity isn't consistently published across all nine leagues.

Where this simplifies

HHI on league titles rewards leagues with more playoff rounds / randomness, not just financial structure — the NFL's single-elimination bracket adds variance a round-robin league wouldn't have. European "revenue sharing" also isn't zero — domestic TV deals are pooled to varying degrees — it's just far smaller and less equalizing than the NFL's near-total pooling. Treat this as directional evidence for the financial-structure hypothesis, not a controlled experiment.

The MLB caveat: Baseball has produced real parity for over a decade — Kansas City (2015), Tampa Bay's playoff run, Milwaukee's contention window — without a hard cap. The Dodgers' 2024–25 back-to-back titles, run on baseball's largest payroll two years running, is the exception the numbers haven't fully caught up to yet. If it continues, MLB's HHI starts drifting toward soccer's territory.
